One sky you can’t recolour
— only make rarer.

Carbon dioxide is invisible; it can’t colour the air. Smoke can. This is the one sky the dial never paints — it only changes how often it arrives.

a labelled illustration, calibrated to real smoke-aerosol levels

What you’re seeing is smoke, not carbon — this sky is rendered at the measured optics of a heavy fire day. Emissions don’t change the colour of clean air; they change how often skies like this arrive. Human-caused warming has roughly doubled the forest area burned across the western US over 1984–2015 (Abatzoglou & Williams, PNAS 2016), and since 2016 wildfire smoke has erased about a quarter of two decades of clean-air progress across most US states (Burke et al., Nature 2023). No single day can be blamed on the total. The trend can.

The dial changes the odds, never the colour — at today’s pace, more seasons like 2020.

A labelled illustration — an event, on demand and reversible, not a permanent state. The sky is rendered at a measured aerosol optical depth — how much light the smoke blocks — calibrated to named sources (AERONET, NASA, NOAA); descriptive, not a claim that CO₂ colours the air on any given day.

Where are you?

This chapter is drawn for one place. Make it yours — every chapter follows.

Your coordinates are rounded to two decimals (about a kilometre) and sent only to Open-Meteo, the weather archive. To name your city we download a list of the world’s cities and find the nearest one on your device — your position is never sent anywhere to be identified. Nothing is stored outside this browser, and it is never added to a link you share.

Each city is a single point, not the whole city, and ERA5’s grid cell is about 28 km across — so the record is that cell’s, not that street’s. The coordinates are echoed when you pick, and all are listed in Bedrock.

Your hot nights. Where you live, the nights are warming faster than the days — and the nights are the dangerous half.

Hot nights are the danger most people miss.

Your body cools down at night to recover. When the nights stop cooling, a heatwave stops giving anyone a break — which is what heat-health research warns about most.

What you can do. The few things one person can do that actually matter — and what has genuinely improved, measured.

Sorted by how far it reaches. The big ones are collective; the personal ones are real but smaller. Both are here on purpose.

Saying so isn’t giving up. It stops “I recycle” standing in for “I asked my representative to act” — and hiding the size of personal cuts turns out to be the less effective move too (Hagmann, Ho & Loewenstein 2019).

The personal levers — real, smaller, fully in your hands

A widely-cited “have one fewer child” figure (58.6 t) is left off on purpose: it attributes a lifetime of descendants’ emissions to a parent, double-counts across generations, and turns a systemic problem into a personal reproductive choice. We don’t think it’s a number anyone can act on honestly.
