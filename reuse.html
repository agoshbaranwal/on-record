Use this. Honestly.

On Record is built to be reused — in a lesson, in an article, in a slide. This page gives you the method in plain words, ready-made embeds, citations you can paste, the licence in one paragraph, and a printable worksheet. Two rules travel with every number: say where it comes from, and describe what changed — never claim one place or one day was caused by climate change.

02 · How every number is made

The method, in plain words

No number on On Record is invented, and none is ours to assert. Each one is a measurement someone else published, that we only counted, averaged, or subtracted — and we show the arithmetic. Here is the whole chain, in order of the site.

The air — CO₂

In plain words

The CO₂ figure in the site's source ledger — about 424 ppm — is how many carbon-dioxide molecules sit in every million molecules of air, measured continuously at Mauna Loa and other baseline stations.

Source

NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory (US government data, free to use with attribution). Last reading shown: 3 September 2026.

What we did to it

Nothing but display the latest published value and its date. The browser re-checks it live on each visit.

The sky — the carbon budget

In plain words

Scientists estimate how much more CO₂ the world can emit before warming is likely to pass 1.5 °C. Two teams publish current figures: about 99 GtCO₂ (Global Carbon Budget 2025) and 102 GtCO₂ (IGCC 2025). At today's rate of about 42 GtCO₂ a year, that is roughly 2.4 years of emissions.

Source

Global Carbon Project (GCB 2025) and Forster et al., Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025 (ESSD). Rate: Global Carbon Budget 2025.

What we did to it

Each budget is published against its own start date. We subtract the CO₂ emitted since that date at the current rate, to bring both to today — and we show that subtraction in the table. "Years left" is simply budget ÷ rate: arithmetic, not a prediction of the future. 1.5 °C is a dial of rising risk, not a cliff.

The ground — Seville's heat

In plain words

In Seville, the number of days each year at or above 35 °C rose from about 34 (1951–1980 average) to about 66 (2016–2025). Nights at or above 25 °C went from roughly 0 to 7 a year. We compare the city only to its own past.

Source

ERA5, the ECMWF's hour-by-hour reconstruction of weather since 1940 (~30 km grid), read via Open-Meteo (CC-BY).

What we did to it

We counted days over each threshold, per year, and averaged across two thirty-year windows. Today's on-site temperature is a live forecast; we measured its bias against the reanalysis (about -0.38 °C) and say so.

The night — Delhi's honest complication

In plain words

Delhi is where we resist a tidy story. Hot nights (≥25 °C) rose from about 109 to 125 a year — but hot days (≥35 °C) edged down, from about 102 to 92. Both are true; we show both.

Source

ERA5 via Open-Meteo (CC-BY), 1951–1980 vs 2016–2025.

What we did to it

The same counting and averaging as Seville. We kept the number that complicates the headline, because a source that refuses to flatter the story is the one worth trusting.

First light — what people believe

In plain words

Across the world, about 89% of people want their government to do more on climate. Yet people guess the number is far lower — nearly everyone underestimates nearly everyone.

Source

Andre, Boneva, Chopra & Falk (2024), Nature Climate Change; per-country figures processed by Our World in Data (CC BY 4.0).

What we did to it

We display the published shares; the exact survey wording is re-verified against the paper at each build. Country examples on the site: India 64% (guessed 36%), Spain 65% (guessed 41%), Indonesia 80% (guessed 44%)..

The cost, and the response

In plain words

One more tonne of CO₂ is estimated to cause about $190 of damage (US EPA, 2023). And the giving lists we link to reckon their best picks avert roughly a tonne of expected CO₂ per dollar — a modelled expectation, not measured tonnes.

Source

US EPA social cost of carbon (Dec 2023, 2% discount rate); Giving Green and the Founders Pledge Climate Fund for the giving figures.

What we did to it

We quote these as the named bodies frame them, with their uncertainty attached. On Record has no donation page of its own — it only links out — so there is no "amount raised" to claim.

The one discipline behind all of it. Every sentence above is descriptive: it says what a record shows. None says a single hot day, or Seville itself, was caused by climate change — no one measurement can carry that claim. When you reuse these numbers, keep that line. 03 · Put a chamber in your page

Embed a chamber

Each chapter of On Record can be embedded on its own, without the site's navigation — just the living sky and that chapter's numbers. Add ?embed=<chamber> to the URL inside an <iframe>. It keeps working when On Record updates overnight, because it loads the live site.

The four narrative chambers are the ones worth framing:

Chapter?embed=What it shows
SkyskyThe world's remaining carbon budget as a living sky, counting down
GroundgroundSeville's heat, every year since 1940, against its own past
NightnightDelhi's hot nights rising while hot days fall
First lightfirstlightThe gap between how much people care and how much we think they do

Morning and Bedrock can be embedded too (the param accepts all six), but they're the action layer and the source ledger — better linked than framed.

· The title, figcaption credit, and ?embed= are the only parts to change.
· loading="lazy" keeps your page fast; the sky only starts when scrolled into view.
· referrerpolicy="no-referrer" and On Record's zero-tracking design mean no reader of your page is ever logged by us — we don't even receive your page's address.
· Embeds are best on a desktop or projector; on a phone, link to the chamber instead: …/?chamber=sky.
· Running a lobby screen or a projector? …/?kiosk=1 cycles the chapters on a calm loop, hands-free.

05 · The licence, in one look

What the licence covers

On Record makes a clean split. What we computed is ours to give away; what we measured belongs to the people who measured it. So the numbers we derived — the counts, the averages, the drawn figures, the arithmetic on the budget — are CC BY 4.0: reuse them anywhere, commercially too, just credit "On Record" with a link. The underlying data is governed by whoever published it; keep their name attached and follow their terms (they're all open).

06 · What we can honestly know

Honest measurement

What we refuse to measure.

We do not know you are here. There is no analytics script on this page, no pixel, no beacon, no cookie set for us to read. We do not count visitors, sessions, or unique devices. We do not know how long you stayed, how far you scrolled, or which chamber held you. We do not know your city, your country, or your network. There is no funnel, no A/B test, no heatmap, no session replay — the code is open, and you can confirm all of this is absent. This page itself stores nothing at all. The instrument remembers only your last visit by default — plus anything you deliberately keep, like a private pledge in your own words or a sky location you chose — all held in this browser alone, sent nowhere, and erasable by clearing the site's storage.

Why. A climate record that surveilled its readers to prove it mattered would be answering the wrong question. The numbers below are the honest ones we can gather without touching you: public traces the code and the archive leave in the open, which anyone can verify against the same public sources. They are small on purpose. A star is a bookmark, not a reader. A fork is a copy, not a use. An archive snapshot is a robot, not a person. A rebuild is our activity, not your attention. None of them count who understood anything, or who did anything — and we would rather show you a true small number than a flattering one we had to watch you to get.

07 · Classroom worksheet

"Describe, don't blame"

One page. Real, sourced numbers from On Record. Prints clean in black and white. Free to copy for any class (CC BY 4.0).

Describe, don't blame

reading the climate record honestly — Name:     Date:  

The one rule. A record can tell you what changed. It cannot, on its own, tell you why any single day happened. Scientists describe what the numbers show; they do not say one hot afternoon "was climate change." Your job today is to spot the difference.

Part A — Read the record.

These figures are real, and each has a source. (Source: ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo; NOAA; Andre et al. 2024. Compiled by On Record, CC BY 4.0.)

Place / thingThenNow
Seville — days ≥ 35 °C per year~34 (1951–1980)~66 (2016–2025)
Seville — nights ≥ 25 °C per year~0~7
Delhi — nights ≥ 25 °C per year~109~125
Delhi — days ≥ 35 °C per year~102~92
CO₂ in the air—~424 ppm
People worldwide who want government action—~89%

Part B — Questions.

Q1. By how many did Seville's days over 35 °C rise between the two periods?  

Q2. Delhi's hot nights went up but its hot days went slightly down. Write one sentence describing this without guessing the cause.
 

Q3. Which of these is a description and which is a claim about cause? Tick.
(a) "Seville now records about 66 days over 35 °C a year, up from 34." ☐ describes ☐ blames
(b) "Today was so hot because of climate change." ☐ describes ☐ blames
(c) "Hot nights in Delhi have become more common over the last 70 years." ☐ describes ☐ blames

Part C — Fix the sentence.

Each line crosses the honest line. Rewrite it as a description of the record, and add where the number comes from.

1. "This heatwave proves the planet is doomed." →  

2. "Delhi is getting cooler in the daytime, so warming isn't real here." →  
(Hint for 2: both numbers are true at once — rising nights, slightly falling days. An honest sentence holds both.)

Part D — Think it through.

· Nearly 9 in 10 people worldwide want more climate action, but most people guess the number is far lower. Why might believing "hardly anyone cares" change what a person does?

· Why is comparing a city to its own past more honest than comparing two different cities?

The takeaway. Say what the record shows. Name where it comes from. Don't turn one day, or one place, into the whole story.

Teacher's key. Q1 ≈ 32 more days (66 − 34). Q3: (a) describes, (b) blames, (c) describes. Part C: rewrite each as a sourced description — e.g. "Records show Delhi's hot nights rose from ~109 to ~125 a year while hot days edged down from ~102 to ~92 (ERA5 via Open-Meteo)."
